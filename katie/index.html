    Read this first

    A brain that holds the thinking behind the programme.

    Not an app. A folder of plain text files on your work Mac, plus an assistant that reads them, writes to them, and asks you the questions you'd otherwise not get around to asking yourself.

    Why you're getting one

    The technology and innovation programme is yours to grow, and most of what shapes it currently lives in three places: the shared notebook, a Google Doc somewhere, and a conversation with me. The first is solid. The second is scattered. The third does not scale, and it makes your programme depend on my availability.

    This is the fix. Your brain arrives already knowing ELC's position on technology, the plan we've adopted, the standards we've committed to, and the decision rules I use when the plan doesn't settle a question. From there it's yours — you add what you find valuable, and it grows on your thinking, not mine.

    I've run this system since May 2026. The parts that survived are the boring ones — one file I read every session, one loop I run every week, one place decisions get written down. Everything clever I built got deleted. This page gives you the surviving parts.

    What it is not

    It is not a replacement for the shared notebook. That stays the curriculum record you and Goldie own together — what gets decided lives there. This is your working surface: where thinking happens before it's decided, and where your own two jobs get held apart. There's a whole chapter on the join later, because getting it wrong is the one way this causes a problem.

    It is also not a place for anything confidential. Chapter 5 is that rule in full, and it is not optional.

    What this actually costs you

    About an hour to build, with me sitting next to you. Then roughly five minutes a day and twenty minutes once a week. If after four weeks it isn't earning that, we stop — that's a real option, not a polite one.

    Step one

    Get the tools on your work Mac.

    Three things, in this order. If any step doesn't look like what's described here, stop and ask me rather than improvising — a wrong turn early is annoying to unpick later.

    1. Sign in to Claude on your ELC account

      Claude Pro, on @elc.ac.th — not a personal account. The vault this builds is a work resource and it should sit behind a work login.

    2. Install Claude Code

      Open Terminal (Command-Space, type "terminal", Enter). A window of plain text appears — that's normal, and you'll only ever type a handful of things into it. Paste this and press Enter:

      curl -fsSL https://claude.ai/install.sh | bash

      Then type claude and press Enter. It will ask you to sign in — use the ELC account from step one.

    3. Make the folder, inside Google Drive

      In Finder, open your Google Drive folder and make a new folder there. That's the whole backup plan: everything the brain writes syncs to Drive automatically, so there is no separate thing to remember.

      Q1What should the folder be called?

      Lowercase, no spaces. You'll type it occasionally, so keep it short.

    4. Point Terminal at it

      Back in Terminal, type cd and a space — then drag the folder from Finder onto the Terminal window and press Enter. That saves you typing the path. Then:

      claude

      You're in. Everything from here happens by talking to it in normal sentences.

    You will not break anything. Every file it makes is plain text, every version is saved, and nothing it does is hidden from you — you can open any of it in Finder and read it like a note.

    Step two

    The shape of it.

    Four ideas. Everything else in this build is one of these four applied somewhere.

    One file it reads every single session

    hot.md — what's live right now. Newest at the top, one block per session. It's the first thing the assistant reads, every time, so you never start a conversation by re-explaining where things are.

    When it gets long, the closed weeks move into recaps/. Stale is worse than empty — a page that's quietly six weeks out of date will confidently tell you the wrong thing.

    One fact, one place

    Every fact lives in exactly one file, and everything else points at it. The moment a number or a date exists in two places, one of them is wrong and you won't know which.

    This is why the brain points at the shared notebook rather than copying from it. Curriculum truth lives there. Your brain holds the thinking and links to the record.

    Written for a machine to retrieve, not a human to admire

    No dashboards, no pretty tables you maintain by hand, no page that exists so somebody can be impressed by it. Dense, current, factual. If nothing would break by deleting a file, delete it.

    It asks, you decide

    The assistant never writes to a file without proposing it first and waiting for your yes. One question at a time — never a list. That rule is what makes it safe to let it touch your work at all.

    Step three

    One word for the next hundred days.

    Every routine checks itself against this word. It's the difference between a brain that files things and a brain that tells you when you're drifting.

    Why one word

    A list of priorities can't referee a decision — everything on it is a priority. One word can. When the week has too much in it, the word is what decides which thing gets dropped, and it makes that call the same way twice.

    Q2Which one?

    These are drawn from what the programme actually needs this year. Pick one, or write your own.

    Q3Say what it means, in one sentence.

    This goes in the rules file and gets read back to you when something's slipping. Write it as a test you could fail, not a value you hold.

    Step four

    The loop that makes it a brain.

    Four routines. You type a slash command, it does the rest. This is the whole system — everything else is furniture.

    /start — the morning brief

    Reads the hot file, sweeps anything you threw in overnight, and gives you a short brief: what's live, what's at risk, what's gone quiet. Read it standing up.

    /close — the grill

    The one that does the real work. It doesn't ask "how was your day" and file the answer — it interrogates you against what's already written down. "You planned three Y5 sessions around the Arduino sequence and the map still says that band is unknown. Which is true?"

    One question at a time, maximum eight, and it writes each answer into the record as it lands. This is where a folder of notes becomes something that knows things.

    /save — after anything that mattered

    A conversation with Goldie, a decision in a meeting, a thread you don't want to lose. It reads back through what you told it and files the decisions, commitments and new names in the right places.

    /week-close and /week-plan

    The week grill asks the honest question: which part of this got no attention at all, and was that a choice or a drift? The plan sets a thesis for the week — one sentence on what the week is actually for — and then walks everything outstanding, one at a time.

    Q4How often should the grill run?

    Your day is classroom-shaped, not meeting-shaped, so a nightly interrogation may be more than the day can feed. Be honest about which of these you'd actually do.

    Q5Which day closes the week?

    The week grill and the week plan can be the same sitting or split across the weekend.

    Q6Anything else worth having on day one?

    All cheap. Anything skipped can be added later by asking Claude for it in plain words.

    Step five

    Where the brain stops.

    This chapter has no questions in it. These rules go into your build whatever you choose elsewhere, because they're what makes a brain on a school machine a reasonable thing to have.

    Never goes in — no exceptions
    • Anything safeguarding. Not a summary, not a note-to-self, not an initial. It has its own route and this is not it.
    • Anything HR. Pay, contracts, performance, anything about a colleague you wouldn't say to their face in a meeting.
    • Named children with anything attached. A child's name plus a difficulty, a diagnosis, a family circumstance — that belongs in the school's systems, not in a folder on your Mac.
    • Anything a parent shared in confidence.

    The test is simple: would you be comfortable if this file were read out? If you hesitate, it doesn't go in.

    The pointer rule — how to keep the useful part

    You often need to remember that something exists without recording what it says. So the brain holds the pointer and the shape, never the contents.

    Write "assistive tech route for one Y2 child — notes in the SSW system, review with the coordinator in October". Not the child, not the condition, not the detail. You keep the thread; the sensitive part stays where it belongs. Your build will have this rule written into its own rules file, and the assistant will push back if you drift over the line.

    Anonymous is fine, and it's most of what you need

    "Three children in the group couldn't get the crocodile clips to hold, so the build stalled before the idea did" is a real, useful observation with nobody identified in it. Almost all pedagogical evidence works like this. Write the pattern, not the person.

    Where the vault lives, and who can see it

    Your ELC Google Drive, on your work account. Not a personal Drive, not iCloud, not Dropbox. It's a work resource on a work machine backed up to a work account — which is what makes it defensible if anyone ever asks, and IT reviews the whole setup before you start.

    Built for your job

    You wear two hats. The brain should know which one you're in.

    You teach K1, and you lead technology integration across the school. Those are two different jobs with two different rhythms, and most of the mess of holding both comes from them being tangled in the same notebook.

    Why split them

    The teaching lane runs daily, is full of specifics, and mostly matters this week. The lead lane runs in months, is full of architecture, and mostly matters next year. Filed together, the second gets buried by the volume of the first — which is exactly how a leadership brief quietly turns into an admin log.

    Split, each lane can be asked its own question. "What's live in K1 this week?" and "What has moved on progression since June?" stop competing for the same shelf.

    Q8When you capture something, how should it decide which lane it belongs in?

    Some things are obviously one or the other. Some — an observation in your own classroom that says something about progression — are genuinely both.

    One thing your brain will deliberately not decide for you

    There are two live answers to how you and Goldie divide the work — the Technology Plan splits you by year band, the brain charter splits you by function. Both are written down and neither has been chosen.

    Your build will hold both and take neither. That call is yours and Goldie's to make together, and a tool that quietly settled it while you weren't looking would be doing something it has no business doing.

    Built for your job

    What it knows before you type anything.

    Your brain doesn't start empty. It starts with the corpus behind the programme — ELC's stated position, the plan, the standards, the sequence we're borrowing from, and the decision rules I use when none of those settle a question.

    How this works

    These live as Google Docs in ELC Drive. Your build's first job is to read them and write itself a short distilled note on each — not copy them. They stay where they are, owned by the school, and your brain points at them. When one gets updated, it's still the source of truth and your brain re-reads it.

    Open any of them now if you want to see what you're inheriting.

    Q9Which of these go in on day one?

    All ticked by default. Untick anything you'd rather come to later — nothing here is load-bearing for the build itself.

    Now the part that makes it yours

    The corpus above is my read of the programme. A brain built only on it would be a very good copy of my thinking, which is not what either of us wants.

    So: add what you find valuable, and say why. The why matters more than the what — a source with a line explaining what you expect to get from it gets used properly. A source dropped in with no note gets cited at random.

    Q10Your own sources.

    Anything: a book you teach from, a framework you trust, a Drive doc, a site, a colleague's planning you learn from. Name it, then say in one line what you want it for.

    Don't try to fill this now. Two or three you already know you want is plenty — the rest arrive when a real question makes you go looking, and that's the right way round.

    Built for your job

    Drafts here. Decided there.

    This is the one rule that keeps your brain and the shared notebook from turning into two competing versions of the truth. It's short, and it's worth reading twice.

    The rule

    Your technology lane is where thinking happens. The shared notebook is where things get decided. Nothing is settled because it's written in your brain — it's settled when it's published into the shared record where Goldie can see it, argue with it, and build on it.

    What goes wrong without it

    Quietly and over about six weeks: the good thinking accumulates in the fast private tool, the shared notebook stops being where the answers are, and Goldie is working from a record that's no longer true. Nobody decides this. It just happens, because the private tool is more convenient.

    So the brain runs the publish step for you — it tracks what's been staged, and at the cadence you set below it shows you the list and asks what's ready.

    Q11How often should it push you to publish?

    This is the moment where private thinking becomes shared record. Too rare and the notebook goes stale; too often and you're publishing half-formed things.

    The other direction, said plainly

    If this system turns out to be a better place for the two of you to work than the shared notebook, that's an allowed outcome — but it's a decision you and Goldie make and write down, not something that's allowed to happen by drift. Your brain will flag it if it notices the shared record going quiet while yours fills up.
